---
title: "On Communication"
date: 2026-06-01
---

Every relationship in my life — with friends, with colleagues, with the people I love, and with myself — rises or falls on how well I communicate. Not how smart I am. Not how hard I work. How well I communicate.

This is something I keep learning the hard way, so I'm writing it down.

---

### Keep calm

The single most disruptive thing I can do in any conversation is to lose composure. When I'm calm, I listen better, I choose words carefully, and I leave room for the other person to be heard. When I'm not, I say things I don't mean, I hear things that weren't said, and I end up solving the wrong problem entirely.

Calm is not passivity. It is the foundation from which anything useful can happen. It takes more strength to stay calm than to react, and the outcomes are almost always better.

---

### Prepare

The conversations that have gone badly for me were rarely the complicated ones — they were the ones I walked into unprepared. I assumed it would sort itself out. It didn't.

Preparing for a conversation is not a sign of distrust or over-thinking. It is respect — for the other person's time, and for the relationship. Think about what I want to say, what I want to understand, and what a good outcome looks like. Even five minutes of this changes everything.

---

### Anxiety is lack of clarity. Fight it with a plan.

When I feel dread before a conversation, it is almost never about the person. It is about the unknown — what will they say, how will they react, what if I say it wrong. That dread is a signal: I haven't thought this through clearly enough.

The antidote is not to avoid the conversation. It is to close the gaps. What is the actual thing I want to say? What am I afraid of? What do I need from this exchange? Writing these down before a hard conversation has never failed to make me feel more prepared, and the conversation itself almost always goes better than I imagined.

Communication gaps — things left unsaid, misunderstandings left unaddressed — compound. They don't resolve on their own. Confronting them early, with clarity and calm, is almost always less painful than letting them grow.

---

None of this is easy. But it's learnable. And it's worth learning, because almost everything good in life depends on it.
